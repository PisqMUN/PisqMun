                  3.5

                  Motions and Precedence

                  A motion is any proposal made by a delegate. It passes with a second and no objections, and the chair can receive no more than three at once. Rules § 7.I

                  1. You proposeA delegate makes the motion.
                  2. Another delegate secondsWithout a second, it cannot pass.
                  3. An objection?No objection: it passes. Objection: it goes to a vote.
                  Waiting for the Secretariat: the rules do not say what vote passes a motion after an objection. Some motions below state their own threshold.

                  Find a motion

                  Which motion comes first?

                  A moderated caucus goes before an unmoderated one. Between two caucuses of the same type, the longer total time goes first. If the total times match, the longer speaking time goes first. The chair can overrule the order. Rules § 7.II

                  Waiting for the Secretariat: the rule on equal caucus lengths is unclearly worded. This is how we read it, so your chair's ruling comes first.
                  3.6

                  Debating a Resolution

                  Once a draft resolution is submitted, the chair sets time for it, time against it, and open debate. Rules § 5.I

                  These motions also apply when debating an amendment. Rules § 7.VI

                  The chair sets the speech length. If time remains after your speech and points of information, the chair may let you yield it to another delegate. Only one yield is permitted, and chairs can overrule yields. Rules § 5.II, 5.III

                    4.1

                    Anatomy of a Resolution

                    A draft resolution has two types of clause: preambulatory clauses explain why the committee is acting, and operative clauses say what it will do. Rules § 4.III.A

                    Rules § 4.III is a rule of the conference. MUN advice is general guidance.

                    This resolution and its submitters are invented for practice, and do not show any country's real position.

                    4.2

                    Preambulatory Clauses

                    These clauses build your argument by citing historical facts, precedents and the purpose of the action. Each one begins with a present participle and ends with a comma. Rules § 4.III.A.1

                    Start with an opening phrase MUN advice

                      Check with your chair: many conferences also accept openings such as “Alarmed by” or “Guided by”. These rules ask for a present participle, so this tool checks for an opening word ending in “ing”.

                      My preambulatory clauses

                        4.3

                        Operative Clauses

                        These are the policies your resolution will create. Each begins with a verb and ends with a semicolon, and the last one ends with a period. They are numbered, in a logical or chronological order. Rules § 4.III.A.2

                        The list offers common operative phrases. Some, such as “Decides”, are usually kept for the UN Security Council, so check with your chair. MUN advice

                            How an unfriendly amendment is debated

                            Waiting for the Secretariat: the rules give two different procedures. Follow your chair until PISQMUN confirms which one applies. Either way, no abstentions are allowed. Rules § 8.III

                            Procedure in § 8.I

                            1. General debate is suspended, and a speakers' list is set up for and against.
                            2. A motion to close debate is in order after two speakers each side, or all speakers on one side and at least two on the other.
                            3. Two speakers oppose the motion, and closure needs a two-thirds vote.
                            4. The committee votes immediately. It passes by simple majority.
                            5. Debate resumes on the general speakers' list.

                            Procedure in § 8.III

                            1. The amendment's submitter may present it and speak for it.
                            2. The chair sets time to debate the amendment.
                            3. When that time ends, the committee votes.
                            4. It passes by a two-thirds majority.

                              5.3

                              Points of Information

                              A point of information asks a delegate with the floor about their speech or position. It must be a question, and it may not interrupt. Rules § 6.IV

                              Is this in order?

                              Doing it well

                              Asking MUN advice

                              • Ask about one thing only.
                              • Choose a gap in the speaker's plan, such as cost or timing.
                              • Keep it short enough to say in one breath.
                              • Use a follow-up only when the debate needs it. Rules § 6.VI

                              Answering MUN advice

                              • Pause, and answer the question you were asked.
                              • If you lack the detail, say what your country would do next.
                              • Link your answer back to your main point.
                              • Stay courteous, even if the question is sharp.
                              5.4

                              Building Coalitions

                              Chairs look for delegates who build alliances and bring others towards shared goals. Use unmoderated caucuses to do it. Award rubric Rules § 4.II.B

                              Your bloc board

                              Moving delegates across the board

                              1. Start with what they need, and listen before you pitch. MUN advice
                              2. Offer a role. Delegates who contribute to the clauses can be co-submitters, but they must then vote for the resolution. Rules § 4.III.B
                              3. Ask undecided delegates to sign. A signatory only wishes to debate it, and does not commit to voting for it. Rules § 4.III.C
                              5.5

                              Negotiation and Compromise

                              Good negotiators balance assertiveness with finding middle ground, and compromise without giving up too much. Award rubric

                              You lead a bloc that wants a deadline for sharing water data. Each choice moves two meters, and your aim is to keep both high. This is practice, so other approaches may also work.

                              5.6

                              Handling Conflict

                              Chairs look for delegates who calm tense situations and respect cultural and political differences. Award rubric

                              1. Pause: do not answer heat with heat.
                              2. Acknowledge: show you heard the concern, even if you disagree.
                              3. Refocus: bring the talk back to the issue, not the person.
                              4. Offer a way forward: suggest wording, a break or a smaller group. MUN advice
                              5.7

                              Consistency and Quality

                              Chairs favour delegates who stay active from beginning to end over those who contribute only now and then, and they look for quality over quantity. Award rubric

                              Plan your sessions

                              Plan at least one contribution for every session. Set the number of sessions when the schedule is confirmed.
